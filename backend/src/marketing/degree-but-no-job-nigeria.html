Overview

You have your degree. You completed NYSC. You have sent dozens of applications. Yet your inbox remains quiet. Maybe you have had one or two interviews that went nowhere, or none at all, despite a second-class upper and a certificate you worked years for. You are not imagining the difficulty, and you are not alone in it — every graduation season in Nigeria adds hundreds of thousands of new degree holders to a labour market that cannot absorb all of them at the pace they arrive. That is not a reflection of your intelligence or effort. It is a structural reality of the market you are entering, and understanding it is the first step toward doing something about it.

This article will not tell you a short course guarantees a job — nobody can honestly promise that. What it will do is explain, plainly, why a degree alone is often not enough to get hired in Nigeria’s current job market, what employers are actually looking for beyond the certificate, and how practical digital skills can help you build a stronger, more demonstrable employability profile.

Why a Degree No Longer Guarantees a Job

Why a degree isn’t enough

Does having a degree guarantee a job in Nigeria? No. A degree confirms that you completed a programme of study and met an academic standard. It does not, by itself, show an employer what you can actually do in a workplace, how you solve problems under pressure, or how comfortable you are with the digital tools most modern jobs now require.

This gap between “qualified on paper” and “ready to contribute” is sharpened here by scale. Nigerian universities and polytechnics graduate very large cohorts each year, competing for a comparatively small number of structured graduate-trainee slots, internships and entry-level roles — particularly in sectors many graduates aspire to, such as oil and gas, banking and telecommunications. When an employer receives hundreds of applications for a handful of positions, and most applicants hold broadly similar qualifications, the degree stops being a differentiator. It becomes the minimum entry requirement, not the deciding factor.

According to Nigeria’s National Bureau of Statistics, the country’s unemployment rate stood at 4.90% in the fourth quarter of 2024, with youth unemployment consistently running higher than the rate for older workers, and time-related underemployment measured separately at 9.2% in the second quarter of that year — a reminder that having some work is not the same as having secure, adequately paid work that matches a graduate’s qualifications. The NBS redesigned its labour force survey methodology in 2023, which lowered the headline unemployment figure compared with earlier years; the number is real, but should be read alongside underemployment data, since plenty of graduates are technically “employed” in roles well below their qualification level.

Qualifications vs. Demonstrated Skills

Qualifications vs. skills

There is a meaningful difference between being qualified and being able to demonstrate what you can do. Qualifications tell an employer what you studied and for how long. Demonstrated skills show an employer what you can produce, fix, analyse or manage — right now, with minimal ramp-up time.

A recruiter screening 300 CVs for a graduate-trainee role cannot interview all 300 people. They are looking for signals that let them shortlist quickly. A degree class is one signal, but it is shared by a large proportion of the applicant pool. What stands out is evidence: a project you built, a campaign you ran, a system you configured, a dataset you analysed, a certificate from a structured course that shows you went beyond the classroom on your own initiative. This is exactly the gap practical digital skills are positioned to fill — not by replacing your degree, but by giving you something concrete to point to when an employer asks, “What can you actually do?”

What Employers Are Actually Screening For

Employers rarely make hiring decisions on a single factor. In practice, most structured recruitment processes — especially graduate-trainee and entry-level hiring in larger Nigerian employers — assess candidates across a combination of eleven things. Notice “degree” is one line of eleven: it remains the entry ticket for most structured programmes, but it rarely wins the seat alone when the competition is this dense.

Academic qualifications

The baseline entry requirement for most structured programmes.

04

05

How you reason through an unfamiliar situation.

06

Whether you understand how the sector you are applying to actually works.

09

10

Evidence of structured learning beyond your degree.

11

Evidence of practical projects

Things you have actually built, run or delivered.

Two Similar CVs, One Difference

A worked example

Picture two graduates applying for the same graduate-trainee role at a Lagos-based bank. Both have a 2:1 in Economics from a reputable Nigerian university. Both completed NYSC. On paper, they look almost identical.

Candidate A’s CV lists their degree, NYSC discharge, and “computer literate.” Candidate B’s CV lists the same degree and NYSC discharge, but adds completion of a structured Digital Marketing course, including a capstone project building a social media campaign plan for a small local business, with measurable objectives and a content calendar they can walk an interviewer through — plus a LinkedIn profile reflecting the same activity.

Neither candidate is guaranteed the job. But when a recruiter is deciding who to invite for an interview, Candidate B has given them a concrete reason to pick up the phone — something specific to ask about, something that suggests initiative and applied capability beyond the classroom.

Building evidence

Building a Digital Portfolio

A digital portfolio is a curated, accessible collection of evidence — projects, certificates, writing samples, campaign plans, data analyses — that demonstrates your practical capability, usually hosted online. For Nigerian graduates, it does not need to be elaborate. It needs to be honest, specific and easy for a recruiter to review in under five minutes.

01

Save everything

Every course project, assignment and capstone you complete, with a short write-up of what you did and why.

02

Keep LinkedIn current

A simple, professional profile updated with your projects, not just your job titles.

03

Run a real (small) campaign

If you learn Digital Marketing or Social Media Management, run one for a friend’s business, a church group or a student organisation, and document the results.

04

Document a security exercise

If you learn Cyber Security Fundamentals, a basic home-network security audit or a phishing-awareness explainer you created.

05

Keep your maps and datasets

If you learn GIS and Drone Mapping, keep the outputs you produce during training as shareable work samples.

Common questions

Frequently asked

Q1

Why can't I get a job even though I have a degree?

A degree confirms academic achievement, but Nigerian employers, especially for competitive graduate-trainee and entry-level roles, typically screen for a combination of qualifications, practical skills, digital competence, communication and demonstrable project experience. In a crowded applicant pool, a degree alone rarely differentiates one candidate from hundreds of similarly qualified others.

Q2

Do I need a master's degree to get hired in Nigeria?

Not necessarily. Many entry-level and graduate-trainee roles are open to first-degree holders. A postgraduate qualification can help in specific fields, but it is not a substitute for demonstrable practical skills, and it carries its own cost and time commitment that should be weighed against other options, such as targeted skills training.

Q3

What skills do Nigerian employers actually want from graduates?

Commonly cited priorities include digital competence, communication, problem-solving, adaptability, professional behaviour and evidence of practical project work, in addition to relevant technical or industry knowledge. Specific digital skills — such as basic cybersecurity awareness, digital marketing, social media management, GIS, renewable energy systems knowledge, or HSE awareness — can be relevant depending on the industry.

Q4

Will a short digital skills course guarantee me a job?

No, and you should be cautious of any provider that claims otherwise. A structured course can help you build genuine skills and demonstrable project evidence, which can strengthen your overall profile, but hiring decisions depend on many factors, including the specific role, the employer and the competitiveness of the applicant pool.

Q5

How is a digital skills course different from what I learned at university?

University programmes are typically broader and more theoretical, covering a discipline in depth over several years. A focused digital skills course is usually shorter, more applied, and centred on a specific practical capability — such as digital marketing execution, GIS software use, or foundational cybersecurity practice — intended to complement, not replace, your degree.

Q6

What is a digital portfolio and do I really need one?

A digital portfolio is an accessible collection of evidence — projects, certificates, writing, campaigns, analyses — that shows what you can do. It is not mandatory for every job application, but for competitive Nigerian graduate roles, it can meaningfully strengthen your CV by giving recruiters something specific and verifiable to review.

Q7

I studied a course unrelated to tech. Can I still learn digital skills like GIS or cybersecurity?

Yes. All six Paleon Training courses are designed for learners regardless of their first degree. Many practical digital skills — including GIS, cybersecurity fundamentals, digital marketing and social media management — do not require a prior technical background to begin learning.

Q8

Does NYSC count as "experience" on my CV?

Yes, your Primary Assignment during NYSC can count as workplace experience, particularly if you took on real responsibilities, contributed to specific outputs, or led any initiative. Describe it with the same specificity you would use for a job — what you did, what tools you used, what the outcome was — rather than listing it as a generic line.

Q9

How long does it take to see results from building a digital portfolio?

There is no fixed timeline, and it depends on your industry, your network, and the broader job market. Building a credible portfolio typically takes weeks to a few months of consistent effort — completing a course, working on projects, and updating your CV and LinkedIn profile — rather than being a one-time task.

Q10

Is it worth paying for a digital skills course if I am already struggling financially after graduation?

This is a genuinely personal decision that depends on your circumstances. Look for a course that is structured, provides real assessments and projects, and is priced transparently, so you can weigh the cost against the practical skills and portfolio evidence you would gain. Compare the time and cost against your other options, such as self-directed free learning, before deciding.

Q11

Can digital skills training help me if I want to apply for postgraduate programmes instead of a job?

It can still be useful. Demonstrable project experience and digital competence are increasingly considered in some postgraduate and scholarship applications, particularly for programmes with a practical or applied component, though requirements vary by institution and programme.
