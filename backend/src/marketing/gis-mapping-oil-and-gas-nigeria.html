How GIS Is Used Across Oil & Gas Operations

A technical guide to where geographic information systems fit across the oil & gas lifecycle — with the data workflow, Nigerian coordinate systems and three illustrative Niger Delta scenarios.

Last reviewed: 24 September 2026 · Reviewed by Paleon Training

Short answer

How is GIS used in oil & gas?

Answer

GIS (Geographic Information Systems) is used in oil & gas to store, analyse and map location-based data across the whole lifecycle. That includes planning exploration surveys, siting wells and flowlines, managing pipeline rights of way, mapping environmental baselines and spills, and planning HSE emergency response. It also covers managing assets and land, and working with host communities. Drones and satellites supply much of the data; GIS turns it into decisions and reports.

Key takeaways

  • GIS supports eight broad areas: exploration, field development, pipelines, environment, HSE, asset management, drone and remote sensing, and land and community management.
  • Every application follows the same workflow: data collection → GIS database → spatial analysis → visualisation → decision support → reporting.
  • Nigerian data often mixes the Minna datum and WGS 84. Getting the coordinate reference system wrong can shift features on the order of a hundred metres.
  • Public Nigerian datasets exist, including NOSDRA's Oil Spill Monitor and its satellite-based Gas Flare Tracker.
  • Drones supplement, but do not replace, regulated land surveying, engineering inspection or statutory integrity checks.

The workflow

From raw data to a decision

Whatever the application, GIS work in oil & gas moves through the same six stages.

01

Data collection

Field GPS/GNSS surveys, drone imagery, satellite imagery (optical and radar), seismic survey positions, engineering drawings, spill and incident records, and community and land records.

02

GIS database

Data is checked, given a correct coordinate reference system, and stored in a structured database (for example a file geodatabase, GeoPackage or PostGIS). This is usually aligned to an industry data model, such as the PODS pipeline data model.

03

Spatial analysis

Buffers around pipelines, overlay of constraints (mangrove, settlements, waterways), proximity and route analysis, terrain analysis from elevation models, and change detection between image dates.

04

Visualisation

Maps, web map dashboards and 3D views that make the analysis readable to engineers, managers, regulators and community representatives.

05

Decision support

Choosing a route, prioritising a patrol, dispatching a response team, scheduling maintenance, or scoping an environmental assessment.

06

Reporting

Regulatory submissions, incident reports, EIA figures, internal management reports, and records that support audit and compliance.

Technical foundations

Coordinate systems and data: the Nigerian specifics

The single most common technical error in Nigerian oil & gas GIS is mixing coordinate reference systems (CRS). Two families are in everyday use:

  • Minna datum (Clarke 1880 ellipsoid) with the Nigeria Transverse Mercator belts — EPSG:26391 West Belt, EPSG:26392 Mid Belt, EPSG:26393 East Belt. Many legacy survey, land and engineering records use these.
  • WGS 84, as used by GPS, most drones and satellite imagery — often projected to UTM. Most of the Niger Delta falls in EPSG:32632 (UTM zone 32N); western Nigeria, including Lagos, falls in zone 31N.

The offset between Minna and WGS 84 is on the order of a hundred metres. Overlaying drone imagery on an untransformed Minna-based pipeline layer can put a line on the wrong side of a creek. Always confirm which datum each dataset uses and which transformation was applied. The EPSG Geodetic Parameter Dataset is the reference for these definitions; it is maintained by the Geomatics Committee of IOGP, the international oil & gas producers' association.

Common formats include vector data (shapefile, GeoPackage, GeoJSON), rasters (GeoTIFF orthomosaics and elevation models) and spatial databases (PostGIS). QGIS (free, open source) and ArcGIS (commercial) are the main desktop platforms — see QGIS vs. ArcGIS for beginners.

Applications

Eight ways GIS is used across oil & gas operations

1. Exploration

Licence and block boundaries, geological maps, existing wells and prior surveys are combined into a basemap. GIS is used to plan seismic survey lines around settlements, waterways and protected areas, and to track survey progress. Geoscientists interpret the subsurface in specialist software. GIS provides the surface context and integrates their results with everything else.

2. Field development

Siting well pads, flow stations and flowline routes is a constraint problem: terrain, flood risk, mangrove and swamp, settlements, access by road or water, and existing infrastructure. Weighted overlay analysis narrows the options before engineers carry out detailed design.

3. Pipeline management

Pipelines are linear assets running through many communities and ecosystems. GIS holds the centreline, right of way (RoW), crossings (rivers, roads, other lines), valve locations, inspection history and incident records. Buffer analysis identifies structures and activities inside the RoW. Comparing imagery across dates helps flag new encroachment or ground disturbance for surveillance teams to verify on the ground.

4. Environmental management

Environmental Impact Assessments need baseline maps of vegetation, water bodies, sensitive habitats and land use. After spills, GIS maps the affected area and nearby sensitive receptors such as mangroves, fishing grounds and water intakes. UNEP's 2011 Environmental Assessment of Ogoniland surveyed 122 km of pipeline rights of way and used remote sensing to link artisanal refining at Bodo West to a roughly 10% loss of healthy mangrove cover between 2007 and 2011. NOSDRA's Gas Flare Tracker estimates flare locations and volumes from VIIRS satellite data.

5. Health, safety and environment (HSE)

Emergency response plans rely on maps of muster points, evacuation and access routes, the nearest medical facilities, and exclusion zones around hazards. Incident and near-miss locations can be mapped to reveal clusters. During a response, a shared map is often the single source of truth for everyone involved.

6. Asset management

Linking each asset (well, valve, tank, cathodic protection test point) to its location, attributes and maintenance history lets teams plan work by area, see overdue inspections on a map, and support integrity management. Industry data models such as PODS standardise how pipeline assets are stored.

7. Drone mapping and remote sensing

Drones capture high-resolution imagery that photogrammetry software turns into orthomosaics and elevation models, useful for site mapping, RoW checks and earthworks monitoring. Satellites cover wider areas: optical imagery for land-cover change, and radar (which sees through cloud) for applications such as oil slick detection on water. Drone flights require compliance with Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority rules (see NCAA drone regulations). Drone data does not replace regulated land surveying by registered surveyors, engineering inspection, or statutory integrity testing; it supplements them.

8. Land and community management

Land acquisition, compensation records, community boundaries and stakeholder locations are all spatial. Under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, operators must set up Host Communities Development Trusts. Mapping which communities host which assets supports that process, as well as consultation and grievance tracking. Community boundaries can be contested, so maps should record the source and status of each boundary rather than presenting one as definitive.

Scenarios

Three illustrative Niger Delta scenarios

These are illustrative composites of typical workflows, not accounts of specific companies or incidents.

Illustrative scenario A · Delta State

Right-of-way encroachment review

A pipeline team holds an older RoW layer in Minna / Nigeria Mid Belt, plus a new drone orthomosaic in WGS 84 / UTM 32N. The GIS analyst transforms the RoW layer to match the imagery, then buffers the centreline to the RoW width. Next they digitise new structures visible in the imagery and run an intersect. The output is a map and a table of possible encroachments, ranked by distance to the pipe, for the RoW and community-relations teams to verify on the ground. The GIS does not decide; it prioritises field checks.

Illustrative scenario B · Bayelsa State

Spill impact and access mapping

After a reported spill at a creek crossing, the response team needs to know what is at risk and how to get there. The analyst plots the spill point and applies a tidal-creek flow direction. They overlay mangrove cover, fishing settlements and water intakes, then map boat access routes and staging points. Satellite imagery from before and after the spill helps estimate the affected shoreline. The maps support the response plan and feed the documentation reviewed during the Joint Investigation Visit (JIV).

Illustrative scenario C · Rivers State

Gas flare and community proximity analysis

An environmental team compares its own flare records with public satellite detections from the NOSDRA Gas Flare Tracker. The analyst maps flare sites, draws distance bands to nearby communities, and tracks detections over time. Discrepancies between internal and satellite records are flagged for investigation. The result supports environmental reporting and flare-reduction planning, and gives community-engagement teams a clear, sourced basis for discussion.

Limits

Limitations and responsible use

  • Accuracy is only as good as the input. Consumer GPS, uncorrected drone imagery and mixed datums all introduce error. Record accuracy with the data.
  • Ground truth still matters. Imagery suggests; field verification confirms.
  • Security. Detailed pipeline and facility maps are sensitive infrastructure data. Access should be controlled, and precise locations should not be published publicly.
  • Regulation. Survey-grade work, drone operations and environmental assessments each have their own legal and professional requirements.

Skills

What skills does this work need?

Entry-level GIS work in oil & gas needs QGIS or ArcGIS fluency, a solid grasp of coordinate systems, basic drone data processing, careful data management and clear map-making. Many roles are open to geography, geology, surveying, environmental science and engineering graduates. GIS is one of the five digital skills Nigerian graduates can use in oil & gas.

Common questions

Frequently asked

Q1

What is GIS used for in the oil & gas industry?

Planning exploration surveys, siting wells and flowlines, managing pipeline rights of way, environmental baselines and spill mapping, HSE emergency planning, asset management, and land and host community management.

Q2

What coordinate system should I use for Niger Delta data?

It depends on the dataset. Legacy records often use the Minna datum with a Nigeria belt projection (EPSG:26391–26393), while GPS, drone and satellite data use WGS 84, often UTM zone 32N (EPSG:32632) in the Niger Delta. Transform everything into one CRS before analysis.

Q3

Can drones replace pipeline inspection or land surveying?

No. Drone imagery supplements regulated land surveying, engineering inspection and statutory integrity testing; it does not replace them. Drone flights must also comply with NCAA regulations.

Q4

Is there public oil & gas GIS data for Nigeria?

Yes. NOSDRA's Nigerian Oil Spill Monitor publishes spill records, and its Gas Flare Tracker publishes satellite-based flare detections. Both are useful for practice projects.

Q5

Which software is used for GIS in oil & gas?

QGIS (free, open source) and ArcGIS (commercial) are the main desktop platforms, with PostGIS for spatial databases and photogrammetry software for processing drone imagery.

Q6

Do I need a degree in geography to work in oil & gas GIS?

Not necessarily. Graduates of geography, geology, surveying, environmental science and engineering commonly enter GIS roles. Demonstrable tool skill and a portfolio project matter, and survey-grade work requires professional registration.
