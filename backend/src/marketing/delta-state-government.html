01The Signal

Delta State's youth unemployment is a state-specific problem, not just a national one.

The National Bureau of Statistics ranks Delta third among South-South states for unemployment, at 40.36% overall with underemployment at 20.1%1. Among young people specifically, the gap to the national picture is wider still.

Delta's labour force numbers 2,494,452, with 1,005,848 currently unemployed1 — a scale no single ministry programme closes on its own. It closes with a structured, repeatable training pipeline that turns graduates already produced by Delta's universities and polytechnics into candidates ministries can actually place.

02The Momentum

Delta State has already proven that structured, digital-era reform works here.

This isn't a cold pitch into an untested idea. The state has already validated both halves of it — fiscal reform and youth ICT training — at smaller scale.

Delta's Internally Generated Revenue grew from about ₦84 billion in 2023 to over ₦200 billion today — a 138% rise achieved through better tax administration and compliance, without raising personal income tax3. That's the same discipline a training pipeline needs: not a bigger budget, a better system. Separately, the state's Ministry of Science and Technology already runs an ICT skills programme for youths aged 20–40 across three tracks — Start-up Academy, ICT for Self-Employment, and Digital Media Skills7. The demand and the appetite are both already demonstrated; what a partnership with Paleon adds is a structured, ministry-mapped curriculum built to scale it faster than an in-house programme can build alone.

03The Case For Digital Skills

Diversifying away from oil requires a workforce that can actually run the alternative.

Delta is already diversifying its economy on paper. Digital skills are what turns that diversification from a budget line into functioning ministries and businesses.

Non-oil activity grew from 41.9% of Delta's GDP in 2013 to 52.5% in 20204, spread across agri-business, light manufacturing, healthcare, tourism, energy and mining — every one of which now runs on digital systems somewhere in its value chain. Nationally, 1.7 million graduates leave Nigerian universities every year8, and the IFC and World Bank project that 230 million jobs across Sub-Saharan Africa will require digital skills by 20308. Delta's own graduates are already part of that 1.7 million — the only open question is whether they enter the workforce trained for it.
