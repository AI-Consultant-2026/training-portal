## A Short History of Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity as a field is younger than you might think. The first computer viruses appeared in the early 1970s — a program called "Creeper" that simply displayed the message "I'm the creeper, catch me if you can" on infected computers. It wasn't malicious. It was almost playful. But it proved a point that we're still dealing with fifty years later: if a computer can run a program, it can run a program you didn't want it to run.

Fast forward to the 1980s and 90s, and we saw the rise of the internet — and with it, the first real wave of malicious hacking. The Morris Worm in 1988 accidentally took down about ten percent of the internet that existed at the time. That single incident led directly to the creation of the first Computer Emergency Response Teams, which still exist today in almost every country, including here in Nigeria with ngCERT.

By the 2000s, cybercrime had become organized. No longer just curious teenagers experimenting — now we had criminal groups, and eventually nation-states, using cyberattacks for financial gain, espionage, and political disruption. Today, cybercrime is estimated to cost the global economy trillions of dollars every year. And here's the part that matters most for you: attackers don't only go after huge multinational companies. Small businesses, local government offices, schools, and hospitals right here in the State are targets too — often because they have weaker defenses and fewer resources to recover.

That's exactly why this course exists. We need more people who understand these threats and know how to defend against them, in every kind of organization, not just the big ones.

## Understanding the Threats

Now let's get practical. If you're going to defend against attacks, you first need to understand the different shapes they take. Today we're going to cover four major categories: malware, ransomware, phishing, and social engineering. These aren't separate, unrelated things — they often work together in a single attack.

### Malware

Malware simply means "malicious software" — any program designed to damage, disrupt, or gain unauthorized access to a computer system. Under this umbrella you'll find viruses, which attach themselves to legitimate files and spread when those files are shared; worms, which spread on their own across networks without needing a human to click anything; trojans, which disguise themselves as legitimate software — think of a "free antivirus" download that's actually spying on you; and spyware, which quietly monitors what you do and sends that information back to an attacker.

The key thing to remember about malware is that it almost always needs a way in. That's called an attack vector, and we'll be using that term constantly throughout this course. An attack vector might be an infected USB drive, a malicious email attachment, an outdated piece of software with a known flaw, or even a fake mobile app.

### Ransomware

Ransomware deserves its own spotlight because it has become the single most financially damaging type of attack over the last decade. Here's how it works: the malware encrypts — essentially locks — all of the files on a victim's computer or network, making them completely unreadable. Then a message appears demanding payment, usually in cryptocurrency, in exchange for the decryption key.

Hospitals, city governments, and businesses of every size have been hit by ransomware, sometimes paying millions of dollars, and sometimes losing everything because paying doesn't always guarantee you get your files back. Later in this course, when we cover incident response, you'll learn exactly what an organization should do — and shouldn't do — when ransomware hits. But for today, just remember: ransomware attacks are almost always preventable through good backup practices and basic security hygiene, which is a phrase you'll hear me repeat a lot.

### Phishing

Phishing is probably the attack type you're most likely to encounter personally, even outside of work. It's a message — usually an email, but increasingly a text message or WhatsApp message — that pretends to be from someone trustworthy: your bank, your employer, a delivery company, even a colleague. The goal is to trick you into clicking a malicious link, downloading an infected file, or typing your password into a fake login page.

What makes phishing so effective isn't clever technology. It's psychology. Good phishing messages create urgency — "your account will be suspended in 24 hours" — or curiosity, or fear. That leads us directly into our final category.

### Social Engineering

Social engineering is the umbrella term for any attack that manipulates human psychology rather than exploiting a technical flaw. Phishing is actually one form of social engineering. Others include pretexting, where an attacker invents a believable scenario — "Hi, this is IT support, I need to verify your password to fix an issue on your account" — and baiting, where an attacker leaves an infected USB drive somewhere it's likely to be found and plugged in out of curiosity.

Here's the uncomfortable truth I want you to sit with: you can install the most expensive, most sophisticated security software in the world, and a single employee clicking the wrong link can undo all of it. That's why, throughout this course, we're going to treat people — not just technology — as a core part of the security picture.
