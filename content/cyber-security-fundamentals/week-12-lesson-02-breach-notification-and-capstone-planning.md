# Lesson 2: Breach Notification and Capstone Planning

Last lesson covered classification and encryption — how to decide what protection data deserves, and how to deliver it. But even strong protection sometimes fails anyway, which raises a different question: what is an organization legally required to do once a breach actually happens? This final lesson answers that, and then turns to the capstone project that ties this entire course together.

## Breach Notification Requirements

Most modern data protection regulations, including GDPR and many U.S. state laws, include specific **breach notification requirements** — legal obligations to notify affected individuals, and often regulators, within a defined time window after a breach involving personal data is discovered.

GDPR is the most commonly cited example: it generally requires notification to the relevant regulator within **seventy-two hours** of an organization becoming aware of a qualifying breach, measured from the moment of discovery, not from whenever the investigation happens to wrap up. Many U.S. state breach notification laws follow a similar logic, though the exact window and who must be notified varies by jurisdiction.

## Why This Connects Back to Incident Response

This is exactly why the incident response planning from Module 5 needs to explicitly account for legal notification requirements in advance, not figure them out for the first time under the pressure of an actual, active incident. A well-prepared organization already knows, before anything goes wrong, exactly who needs to be notified, within what timeframe, and through what process — legal counsel, affected customers, regulators, sometimes the press.

An incident response plan that handles containment and recovery well but never mentions notification deadlines is an incomplete plan. The clock on a GDPR-style window starts the moment the breach is discovered, regardless of how prepared or unprepared the organization is to meet it.

## Preparing Your Capstone Project

Your capstone project asks you to act as a security consultant for a mid-sized manufacturing company here in the State that's had a minor security incident and wants to seriously improve its cybersecurity posture. You'll need to produce a security audit report, a remediation roadmap, an incident response plan, and a final presentation.

This week's assignment has you submit and get your project proposal approved before you begin serious work. Use this proposal stage well: think carefully about scope, be realistic and specific about what a fifty-employee manufacturing company with a limited IT budget can genuinely, practically implement, and don't be afraid to ask for feedback before diving into the full deliverables.

## Treat It as One Connected Story

Here's a genuinely useful piece of practical advice: don't treat the capstone as twelve separate, disconnected topics crammed into one project. Treat it as one connected story. Your audit findings — informed by everything from Module 1's threat landscape through Module 3's vulnerability assessment techniques — should directly drive your remediation roadmap. Your risk assessment work from Module 4 should directly justify why certain remediation items are prioritized ahead of others. Your incident response plan should build on the lifecycle from Module 5, tailored to this company's actual environment, and now explicitly include the notification requirements covered earlier in this lesson.

Your final presentation should tell that entire story clearly to an audience of business leaders who may not have a deep technical background — remember the reporting and communication principles from Module 3.

That's the complete arc of this course: the foundational threat landscape, network security, ethical hacking, risk management and compliance, incident response and forensics, and finally, cloud and data security. Every module builds on the ones before it, and the capstone project is your opportunity to demonstrate that connected understanding in one comprehensive, practical piece of work — genuinely representative of what security consulting looks like in the real world.

Making it through twelve weeks of genuinely substantial material means you already have a working foundation many people never take the time to build.
