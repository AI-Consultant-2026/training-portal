# Video Script: History of Cybersecurity — How Threats and Defenses Have Evolved

## [0:00–0:45] Cold open

Text types out on screen, letter by letter, like a real 1971 teletype.]

"I'M THE CREEPER. CATCH ME IF YOU CAN."

[HOLD on that line for two full seconds. Then cut hard to host, direct to camera.]

That sentence is fifty-five years old. No malware, no ransom, no theft — just a program, quietly proving a point we are still dealing with today: if a computer can run instructions, it can run instructions nobody asked it to run.

## [0:45–2:15] Why this history matters

Welcome to the History of Cybersecurity. I'm [YOUR NAME]. Over the next half hour, we're going to walk the entire arc — from the first program that ever moved itself between computers, to the ransomware gangs and state-sponsored operations making headlines this year.

I want to be upfront about why we're spending a full lesson on history before you touch a single tool. It's not trivia. **Every major idea in modern cybersecurity is a direct answer to something that already went wrong.** Firewalls exist because networks got attacked. Antivirus software exists because viruses spread faster than people could manually clean them up. Zero trust architecture exists because "trust everything inside the network perimeter" got exploited again and again. If you understand the failure that produced a defense, you understand *why* that defense is shaped the way it is — and you'll be far better at reasoning about the next attack nobody's invented yet.

So think of this less as a history lecture and more as watching the entire discipline of cybersecurity being built, brick by brick, under fire. And notice, as we go, that almost none of these turning points came from a genius working alone in secret. They came from ordinary curiosity, ordinary greed, ordinary mistakes — a locked door, a shared password file, a pirated floppy disk, one tiny bug in one small library nobody thought to double-check. The technology keeps changing. The human patterns behind it barely do at all.

## [2:15–5:30] Before the internet: 1940s–1971

[CUT TO: black-and-white photo style graphic — a room-sized mainframe computer]

Computer security didn't start with hackers. It started with **physical access**. In the 1940s and 50s, computers were enormous, room-filling machines, and "security" simply meant a locked door and a guest list. If you weren't physically standing in the room, you posed no threat at all — there was nothing to connect to from the outside.

That started to change as computers became **time-shared**. Systems like MIT's Compatible Time-Sharing System, built in the early 1960s, let multiple people use terminals connected to the same central machine at the same time — each person genuinely believing they had the computer to themselves. That single design shift created a brand-new question that had simply never needed asking before: how do you stop one legitimate user from reading, or damaging, another legitimate user's data on a machine they're both allowed to use? Not an intruder. A regular user, doing something they weren't supposed to be able to do. This is genuinely the birth of computer security as its own distinct discipline, separate from physical security — and, fittingly, one of the very first recorded "hacks" was students at MIT figuring out how to read the master password file on that exact system.

In 1970, the RAND Corporation published what's often considered the first systematic treatment of the problem — a report authored by Willis Ware, examining how to protect classified information on shared computer systems. Three years later, two researchers named David Bell and Leonard LaPadula formalized what became known as the **Bell-LaPadula model** — a mathematical framework for confidentiality, built for the US Department of Defense. Neither of these was flashy. Neither involved an attacker. But both represent something crucial: security was being treated as a discipline you could reason about formally, before it was ever treated as a battle you had to fight reactively.

Then, in 1969, the **ARPANET** went live — the direct ancestor of the modern internet, connecting a handful of university and research computers together for the first time. And once computers could talk to each other, everything changed.

## [5:30–10:00] The first self-moving program, and the first PC viruses: 1971–1986

In 1971, a researcher named Bob Thomas, working at the company BBN, wrote an experimental program called **Creeper**. It ran on the ARPANET, and it did something no program had done in quite this way before: it moved itself from computer to computer, displaying that message you saw at the very start of this video — "I'm the creeper, catch me if you can." It didn't steal anything. It didn't damage anything. It was, genuinely, an experiment in self-replicating, self-moving code.

And in response, another BBN researcher, Ray Tomlinson — the same person credited with inventing email — wrote a second program called **Reaper**, whose entire job was to chase Creeper through the network and delete it. Stop and appreciate what just happened: the very first self-propagating program on a network was immediately followed by the very first program written specifically to hunt down and remove it. Attack and defense, born as twins, in 1971. That pattern — a new capability appears, and a defense gets built in direct response — is going to repeat, over and over, for the rest of this video.

Through the rest of the 1970s, the more common threat wasn't software at all — it was **phone phreaking**: exploiting the analog tone-based signaling system that controlled the telephone network to make free long-distance calls. The most famous phreaker, John Draper, discovered that a toy whistle given away in cereal boxes happened to produce the exact 2600 Hz tone used to access AT&T's long-distance switching system. It sounds almost quaint today, but phone phreaking culture directly fed into the early hacker culture of the 1980s — many of the same people, the same curiosity about how systems really worked underneath their official rules.

Personal computers changed the threat model entirely. In 1982, a fifteen-year-old named **Rich Skrenta** wrote **Elk Cloner**, widely considered the first computer virus to spread in the wild outside a lab. It infected Apple II computers through floppy disks — when you booted from an infected disk, the virus copied itself onto the memory, and then quietly infected the next clean disk you inserted. Every fiftieth boot, it displayed a short, mostly harmless poem. No network was even required — the entire spread mechanism was people physically sharing floppy disks.

Then, in January 1986, two brothers in Lahore, Pakistan — Basit and Amjad Farooq Alvi — released a program called **Brain**, the first virus to target IBM-compatible PCs running MS-DOS, which by then were becoming the dominant business computer. Their stated motive wasn't malice — they'd written medical software and were frustrated by customers pirating it, so Brain was designed to infect pirated copies and, notably, actually included their real names, address, and phone number in the code, expecting people to call them about it. People did call — from all over the world — and the brothers ended up fielding a global wave of confused, sometimes angry phone calls about a virus that had spread vastly further than they ever anticipated.

There's one more idea from this exact period worth planting now, because we're going to come back to it again and again later in this video. In 1984, computer scientist **Ken Thompson**, co-creator of the Unix operating system, gave a lecture called "Reflections on Trusting Trust." His argument, boiled down, was unsettling: if an attacker can compromise the *compiler* — the tool used to build all your other software — they can make it silently insert a backdoor into everything compiled with it, including future versions of the compiler itself, with no trace left in any source code a human could ever read or audit. In 1984 this was a thought experiment. Today, it's essentially a precise description of how modern **supply-chain attacks** work — compromise one trusted, upstream piece of software, and every single thing built on top of it inherits the compromise. Keep that idea in your back pocket.

## [10:00–14:30] The Morris Worm and the birth of formal incident response: 1986–1995

[CUT TO: graphic — a simple network diagram, nodes lighting up rapidly in sequence]

That same year, an astronomer named **Clifford Stoll**, managing computers at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, noticed a 75-cent accounting discrepancy in computer usage logs. Instead of writing it off, he investigated — and traced it to an unauthorized user quietly moving through his network. Stoll spent months tracking this intruder, eventually discovering the attacker, Markus Hess, was breaking into US military and research computers and selling what he found to the KGB. Stoll wrote the whole story up in a book called *The Cuckoo's Egg*, and it remains genuinely worth reading — it's one of the first real accounts of methodical incident response and digital forensics, years before either term was in common use.

Then came the moment that, more than any other, turned computer security from a niche technical concern into a matter of urgent public policy: **November 2, 1988**. A Cornell graduate student named **Robert Tappan Morris** released a self-replicating program onto the fledgling internet. He later claimed it was intended purely as a research experiment to gauge the size of the internet — but a bug in his code caused it to re-infect the same machines over and over, at a runaway pace. Within hours, the **Morris Worm** had infected roughly ten percent of every computer then connected to the internet — around six thousand machines, which sounds small today but was a genuinely massive fraction of the entire network at the time. Universities and research institutions across the country ground to a halt.

The fallout was immediate and structural. Within weeks, Carnegie Mellon University, with funding from the US government, established the world's first **Computer Emergency Response Team**, CERT/CC — a permanent, coordinated body for tracking vulnerabilities and coordinating responses to major incidents. That single organizational innovation echoes directly into today's national CERTs found in nearly every country, including ngCERT here in Nigeria. And Morris himself became the first person convicted under the newly passed **Computer Fraud and Abuse Act** of 1986 — American law had, for the first time, explicitly caught up to the idea that breaking into a computer system was a crime in its own right, not just a means to some other, older crime like theft or fraud.

The very next year, in 1989, a biologist named Joseph Popp mailed roughly twenty thousand floppy disks, labeled as an AIDS-risk-assessment questionnaire, to attendees of a World Health Organization conference and to addresses pulled from a magazine subscriber list. Buried inside was what's now recognized as the **first documented ransomware**, later nicknamed the "AIDS Trojan" or PC Cyborg. After a set number of reboots, it silently encrypted file names on the victim's hard drive and displayed a demand to mail payment to a post office box in Panama to restore access. The encryption itself was crude and was broken quickly — but the entire business model that would define CryptoLocker and WannaCry decades later was already fully formed, right here, in 1989: lock the victim's own data, then demand payment for its return.

The early-to-mid 1990s brought the internet out of research labs and into homes and businesses, and the security industry professionalized rapidly to keep pace. In 1994, an Israeli company called Check Point released **FireWall-1**, widely credited as the first commercially successful **stateful inspection firewall** — a device that didn't just block traffic based on simple rules, but actually tracked the state of ongoing connections to make smarter decisions. Antivirus companies that still dominate the market today, like McAfee and what became Norton, matured through this same period, racing to keep up with a catalog of known viruses that was, for the first time, growing faster than any one person could track by hand — which is exactly why antivirus software shifted from something a technician ran occasionally to something that had to update itself automatically, every single day, just to keep pace.

It's also worth naming the quieter, less dramatic side of this decade, because it matters just as much as any single attack: standards bodies began formalizing what "good security" even meant in the first place. The ISO 27001 information security standard traces its roots to a British standard published in 1995, and organizations increasingly needed to *prove*, on paper, that they were managing security properly — not just claim it informally. Security was becoming something you could be audited on, not just something you did.

And in February 1995, one of the most-publicized hacker manhunts of the era ended when **Kevin Mitnick** was arrested, after a pursuit that involved security researcher Tsutomu Shimomura personally tracking him down. Mitnick's case, more than perhaps any other single event, shaped the public's image of "the hacker" for a generation — and, after serving his sentence, he went on to become a security consultant himself, which tells you something important we'll return to throughout this course: the line between attacker mindset and defender mindset is often thinner, and more useful, than people assume.

This same period also gave the security world something it had never really had before: a community, meeting in person, on purpose. **DEF CON**, founded in Las Vegas in 1993, and **Black Hat**, founded in 1997, turned what had largely been an isolated, scattered set of individual hackers and researchers into an actual professional field with annual conferences, shared research, and, crucially, a norm of **responsible disclosure** — the practice of privately telling a vendor about a vulnerability and giving them real time to fix it, before ever making it public. That norm alone prevented an enormous number of vulnerabilities from being weaponized the moment they were found, and it's a professional courtesy this entire industry still runs on today.

## [14:30–19:30] The worm era goes global, and cybercrime gets organized: 1999–2008

By the end of the 1990s, email had become the new favorite delivery mechanism. In March 1999, the **Melissa virus** — a Microsoft Word macro that emailed itself to the first fifty contacts in a victim's address book — spread so fast it forced major companies, including Microsoft itself, to temporarily shut down their email systems entirely just to contain it.

Then, in May 2000, came **ILOVEYOU** — an email worm out of the Philippines, written by a student named Onel de Guzman, disguised as a love letter attachment. It's estimated to have caused upward of ten billion dollars in global damage, and it exposed a huge, simple weakness: people will click almost anything if the subject line is compelling enough. That's a lesson about human psychology, not technology, and it's one this entire field is still relearning today.

[CUT TO: graphic — a world map, red dots spreading outward in a fast pulse]

2001 brought two fast-spreading worms, **Code Red** and **Nimda**, both exploiting flaws in Microsoft's web server software to deface websites and spread automatically, no email click required at all. Then, in January 2003, **SQL Slammer** set a record that's honestly still remarkable: it doubled the number of infected machines every roughly eight and a half seconds, and within about ten minutes had infected the vast majority of vulnerable servers that existed anywhere on the internet. It briefly knocked out South Korea's internet almost entirely and disrupted services as varied as airline check-in systems and bank ATMs. Slammer proved that a worm could now move faster than any human response team could possibly react — defense had to become automated, or it couldn't work at all.

Through the mid-2000s, something important shifted underneath all of this: cybercrime stopped being mostly about curiosity or notoriety, and became **organized and financially motivated**. Botnets — networks of thousands or millions of quietly infected computers, controlled remotely — became a genuine criminal business model, rented out for spam campaigns and early denial-of-service attacks. The 2008 **Conficker** worm built one of the largest botnets ever seen, ultimately infecting an estimated nine to fifteen million computers worldwide, and its creators used increasingly sophisticated techniques just to keep security researchers from shutting their command infrastructure down.

This same period gave rise to a very different kind of actor: in 2008, a loosely organized collective calling itself **Anonymous** ran its first major coordinated public campaign, targeting the Church of Scientology. It marked the arrival of **hacktivism** — attacks motivated by political or social causes rather than direct financial gain — as a recognized category alongside crime and espionage. And in 2004, the payment card industry, responding to a steady rise in card fraud, introduced the **PCI Data Security Standard**, one of the first major industry-wide compliance frameworks explicitly built around protecting sensitive financial data — a model that later inspired far broader regulation.

## [19:30–24:30] Cyberweapons and the age of the mega-breach: 2010–2017

[CUT TO: graphic — an industrial facility silhouette, a single red warning icon over one component]

In June 2010, security researchers uncovered something categorically new: a piece of malware called **Stuxnet**, engineered with extraordinary precision to seek out one very specific configuration of industrial control equipment — the centrifuges at Iran's Natanz uranium enrichment facility — and subtly cause them to spin themselves apart, all while feeding false, normal-looking readings back to the human operators watching the monitors. Stuxnet is widely reported to have been a joint US-Israeli operation, though it's never been officially confirmed by either government. What matters for our purposes is this: for the first time, malicious code had caused real, physical, mechanical destruction. The line between "cybercrime" and "cyberwarfare" had been crossed, permanently, in public view.

The years that followed brought a wave of breaches large enough to become genuinely mainstream news, not just industry news. In December 2013, **Target** disclosed that attackers had stolen roughly forty million customers' payment card details — not through Target's own systems directly, but through stolen credentials from a small HVAC contractor with network access. It became the textbook example of **third-party and supply-chain risk**: your security is only as strong as every vendor you've connected to your network. In November 2014, **Sony Pictures** was hit by a devastating attack, attributed to North Korea and tied to the studio's planned release of a film satirizing that country's leadership — attackers didn't just steal data, they deployed destructive malware that wiped systems and leaked enormously embarrassing internal emails. And in 2015, the US **Office of Personnel Management** disclosed that background-investigation records — the kind used for security clearances — for over twenty million people had been stolen, an intelligence goldmine attributed to Chinese state-linked actors.

And in April 2014, a vulnerability called **Heartbleed** was disclosed in **OpenSSL** — the open-source encryption library that a huge share of the entire internet quietly relied on to secure supposedly private connections. A tiny programming mistake meant an attacker could remotely trick a vulnerable server into leaking small chunks of its own working memory — sometimes containing passwords, private encryption keys, or session data — without leaving any trace in the server's logs at all. Heartbleed forced an enormous, coordinated scramble to patch servers and reissue security certificates across the entire internet, and it raised a question this field still hasn't fully answered: an enormous amount of the software the whole internet depends on is free, open-source, and maintained by a small number of volunteers. What happens when one of those small, quietly load-bearing projects has a critical flaw? Hold onto that question — we're going to see it resurface, at even larger scale, before this video ends.

Running in parallel to all of this, a new criminal business model was quietly maturing: **ransomware**. September 2013's **CryptoLocker** is widely considered the template — strong encryption locking victims' files, a countdown timer, and payment demanded in Bitcoin, still new enough at the time to feel almost exotic. It was disrupted in 2014, but the model it proved out — encrypt, extort, collect anonymously — never went away. It came back, at global scale, in May 2017, with **WannaCry**: a ransomware worm that spread using a Windows vulnerability called **EternalBlue**, a tool originally developed by the US National Security Agency and then stolen and leaked publicly by a group calling itself the Shadow Brokers. WannaCry infected more than two hundred thousand computers across roughly one hundred fifty countries within days, and notably crippled parts of the United Kingdom's National Health Service, delaying real medical care. It was ultimately slowed by a security researcher who discovered, almost by accident, a hidden kill-switch domain buried in the code.

Barely a month later, in June 2017, came **NotPetya** — disguised to look like ransomware, but actually a purely destructive wiper with no real way to pay for recovery at all. It spread through a compromised update to a piece of Ukrainian accounting software, and while Ukraine bore the brunt of it, the damage spilled globally — shipping giant Maersk, pharmaceutical company Merck, and logistics firm FedEx's European division were all hit hard enough that total global damages are estimated at over ten billion dollars, making it, at the time, the most costly cyberattack in history. It's widely attributed to Russian state-linked actors targeting Ukraine specifically.

## [24:30–27:30] Regulation catches up, and the perimeter dissolves: 2010s–2020

All of this damage produced a real regulatory response. Around 2010, an analyst named John Kindervag, then at Forrester, coined the term **zero trust** — the principle that no device or user should be automatically trusted just because it's inside the corporate network perimeter; every single request has to be verified, every time. It took roughly a decade to become mainstream practice, formalized by a US government standards document in 2020, but it represents a genuine philosophical shift: the old model assumed a strong outer wall was enough. The new model assumes attackers are already inside, and builds defense accordingly.

And in May 2018, the European Union's **General Data Protection Regulation**, GDPR, took effect — establishing serious, enforceable financial penalties for organizations that fail to adequately protect personal data. It became a global template; companies far outside Europe adjusted their security practices simply because they had European customers, and similar regulations have followed in other regions since.

## [27:30–29:30] Where we are now

[CUT TO: host, direct to camera, slightly closer framing for the closing section]

Which brings us to the world you're training to defend right now. In December 2020, the **SolarWinds** attack showed just how far supply-chain compromise could reach: attackers, attributed to Russian intelligence, quietly inserted malicious code into a routine software update for a widely used network monitoring tool, and rode that single compromised update into the networks of multiple US government agencies and hundreds of major companies, undetected for months. In May 2021, the **Colonial Pipeline** ransomware attack shut down a major fuel pipeline on the US East Coast for days, triggering real gas shortages and panic buying — and suddenly ransomware wasn't just an IT problem, it was front-page news and a national security conversation. And in December 2021, remember that question we held onto from Heartbleed? Here's the answer, at a scale nobody had quite anticipated: a vulnerability called **Log4Shell** was found in **Log4j**, a small, unglamorous, open-source logging utility used quietly inside an enormous share of the world's Java-based software — everything from cloud platforms to enterprise applications to, reportedly, even some video games. One flaw in one small library forced an enormous portion of the entire internet into simultaneous emergency patching, precisely because almost nobody, including many large, well-resourced organizations, had ever actually mapped out just how deep into their own systems that one small, trusted piece of code had quietly spread.

Today, ransomware has industrialized into **ransomware-as-a-service** — established criminal groups, names like LockBit, Conti, and REvil among the most notorious of the last few years, rent out their encryption tools, their leak-site infrastructure, and even their customer-support-style negotiation chat systems to other criminals, taking a cut of every ransom paid, the same way a legitimate software company might license its product to resellers. And the tactics have hardened further into what's often called **double extortion**: attackers don't just encrypt your data anymore, they quietly steal a copy of it first, and threaten to publish it publicly no matter what you do — which means even a perfect, untouched backup no longer fully protects you, because the threat has shifted from "you can't get your data back" to "everyone is about to see it."

The defensive side is racing to keep pace with the very same tools attackers are starting to use. Artificial intelligence is being used to write far more convincing phishing emails, translate scams fluently into any language, and even generate convincing fake voices and video for social engineering — and it's being deployed, just as urgently, on the defensive side, to sift through millions of daily security alerts and flag the handful that actually matter, faster than any human analyst team could alone. Multi-factor authentication, and newer, phishing-resistant **passkey** technology built to replace passwords entirely, are being pushed hard by every major technology company specifically because the plain password — something you type, something that can be guessed, stolen, or phished — has now failed, repeatedly and expensively, for fifty straight years.

## [29:30–30:00] Closing

Notice the pattern one more time, because it's the entire point of this lesson. Creeper appeared in 1971, and Reaper was built, within the same year, to answer it. The Morris Worm hit in 1988, and CERT/CC was built, within weeks, to answer it. Firewalls answered network intrusion. Antivirus answered the first wave of viruses. Zero trust answered the failure of the old network perimeter. Regulation answered a decade of breaches nobody was being held accountable for. Every single defense you're going to learn across the rest of this course exists because something specific, real, and often very expensive went wrong first — and somebody, usually under real pressure, had to build the answer.

That's genuinely the whole shape of this field, condensed into one sentence: **attackers find a new way in, defenders build a specific answer, and the next attacker has to find something newer still.** You're not just learning a toolkit of firewalls and passwords and encryption. You're joining a fifty-year-old conversation between attackers and defenders that is still very much ongoing — and after today, you understand exactly how we got here, which means you're already better equipped to recognize where it's headed next.

Thanks for watching. I'll see you in the next lesson.
