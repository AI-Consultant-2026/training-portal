# Lesson 2: Conducting a Post-Incident Review

Last lesson covered the technical discipline of understanding exactly what happened during an incident. This lesson covers what an organization does with that understanding — the "lessons learned" phase mentioned back in Module 5's six-phase lifecycle, and the one most often skipped under time pressure.

## When to Hold It

A post-incident review should ideally happen soon after an incident is fully resolved — close enough that details are still fresh in everyone's memory, but far enough removed that the team isn't still operating in crisis mode. Too soon, and people are exhausted and still reactive. Too late, and the details that actually matter have already faded or been reconstructed inaccurately from memory.

## What a Good Review Includes

Beyond a clear, factual timeline of what happened, a good post-incident review covers what worked well, what didn't, and — critically — specific, concrete action items. "We should communicate better next time" is not an action item; "add a dedicated incident Slack channel that auto-invites the on-call rotation" is. Vague intentions rarely survive contact with the next normal, busy week; specific commitments with an owner attached actually get done.

## Blameless, Not Toothless

The tone of an effective post-incident review is **blameless**. That doesn't mean nothing matters or nobody is accountable — it means the review focuses on systems and processes rather than hunting for an individual to pin the incident on.

This isn't just a nicer way to run a meeting; it's a practical necessity. Punishing people for reporting incidents, or for having been involved in one, creates a powerful incentive to hide problems rather than surface them quickly — exactly the opposite of what an organization needs during an active incident, when speed of reporting is what limits damage. A team that's afraid of blame quietly works around problems instead of flagging them, and an organization only finds out how bad things really were once it's too late to respond well.

## Documentation Is the Work, Not a Chore Layered On Top

It's easy to treat detailed documentation as a bureaucratic add-on to the "real" security work. It isn't. Meticulous documentation — of forensic findings, of chain of custody, of a post-incident review's timeline and action items — genuinely is good security work, not something separate from it. An incident that's handled brilliantly but never properly documented teaches an organization nothing; the next team facing a similar incident starts from zero all over again.

## Bringing It Together

This week's assignment asks you to write a complete incident report based on a scenario, applying exactly the discipline covered across both lessons: a clear factual timeline, and a professional, blameless tone. Together, digital forensics and post-incident review close the loop that Module 5 opened — from the moment an incident is detected, all the way through to an organization that's measurably better prepared for the next one.
