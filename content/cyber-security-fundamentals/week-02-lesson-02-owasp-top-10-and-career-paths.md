# Lesson 2: The OWASP Top 10 and Career Paths

Let's talk about a resource you'll hear referenced constantly — not just in this course, but throughout your entire career: the **OWASP Top 10**. Then we'll close out the week by talking about where all of this can actually take you.

## A Resource You'll Hear For the Rest of Your Career

OWASP stands for the **Open Web Application Security Project** — a nonprofit that has spent over two decades studying real-world security incidents and publishing free, practical guidance. Their most famous publication is the OWASP Top 10, a regularly updated list of the ten most critical security risks facing web applications.

We won't walk through all ten today — several of them, like injection attacks and broken access control, get covered in real depth once you reach ethical hacking later in this course. What matters right now is understanding *why* a list like this exists: the same handful of mistakes get made, over and over, by different organizations, in different countries, in different industries. Learn to recognize these patterns now, and you'll spend the rest of your career spotting them everywhere — because they really are everywhere.

## Three Patterns Worth Knowing Right Now

- **Broken access control** — a system fails to properly check whether a user is allowed to do something before letting them do it.
- **Security misconfiguration** — a system is left with default passwords, unnecessary features enabled, or overly permissive settings.
- **Vulnerable and outdated components** — an organization is running old software with known, publicly documented flaws that nobody has bothered to patch.

Notice something? None of these require an attacker to be a genius. Most successful attacks exploit basic oversights, not brilliant hacking. That should be reassuring: it means disciplined, careful defenders can prevent the overwhelming majority of attacks just by doing the fundamentals well.

## Where This Can Take You

Cybersecurity isn't one job — it's an entire industry with several genuinely different paths, and they don't all require the same skills or personality type.

- **Security engineering** — designing and implementing the actual defenses: firewalls, encryption systems, secure network architecture. A fit if you enjoy building and configuring things.
- **Penetration testing** — legally, ethically breaking into systems to find weaknesses before criminals do. A fit if you're naturally curious and enjoy a puzzle.
- **Incident response & digital forensics** — investigating what happened after a breach and helping an organization recover. A fit if you're detail-oriented and calm under pressure.
- **Governance, risk, and compliance (GRC)** — policy, documentation, and working across an organization. A fit if you enjoy people and process more than deep technical hacking.
- **Security operations** — monitoring systems for signs of an ongoing attack. A fit if you enjoy analyzing large amounts of data and spotting patterns.

There is no single "correct" way to be good at cybersecurity. As you move through the rest of this course, pay attention to which topics genuinely pull at your attention — that's real information about where you might want to specialize.

## This Week's Work

Your assignment this week is a personal cybersecurity awareness checklist — practical things anyone can do to protect themselves: strong, unique passwords, multi-factor authentication, skepticism of unexpected messages, keeping software updated. Write it as if you were teaching a family member who has never thought about this before. Translating technical knowledge into plain language is one of the most valuable, most underrated skills in this entire industry — and it's a skill every one of the five paths above actually needs.
