# Week 6 Lecture Script: Paying to Be Seen

[Open direct to camera]

Welcome to Module 4. Everything we've covered so far — strategy, content creation, community management — falls under "organic" social media: growth and reach earned without direct payment. This week, we cover paid advertising, which for most businesses eventually becomes an essential complement to organic effort, since platform algorithms increasingly limit organic reach in order to encourage advertising spend.

## Ad Platform Features

Each major platform offers its own dedicated advertising system, and understanding their distinct strengths matters for making smart platform choices, building directly on the platform comparison work from week one.

**Facebook Ads Manager** is genuinely one of the most powerful and detailed advertising platforms available anywhere, and importantly, it also controls advertising for Instagram, since Meta owns both platforms. It offers extremely granular targeting options and a wide range of campaign objectives suited to businesses of essentially any size.

**Instagram Ads**, run through that same Ads Manager, integrate naturally into feeds, Stories, and Reels, and tend to perform especially well for visually strong products and lifestyle brands.

**LinkedIn Ads** are considerably more expensive per click than the other platforms, but they offer uniquely valuable professional targeting — by specific job title, industry, or company size — making them worthwhile specifically for B2B businesses and professional services, despite the higher cost.

**TikTok Ads** lean heavily into native, authentic-feeling short-form video content, and tend to substantially underperform when an advertiser simply repurposes a traditional, polished, obviously "ad-like" video rather than genuinely creating something in TikTok's fast, native style.

## Campaign Objectives and Structure

Every ad platform organizes campaigns around a similar underlying hierarchy, and understanding this structure is essential before you spend any actual money.

At the top sits the **campaign**, where you select an overall objective — awareness, traffic, engagement, lead generation, or conversions, deliberately connecting directly back to the specific business goals we discussed in week one. Within a campaign sit one or more **ad sets**, each defining a specific audience, budget, and schedule. Within each ad set sit the actual **ads** themselves — the specific creative, meaning the images, video, and copy that a real person actually sees.

Choosing the right campaign objective genuinely matters, beyond it just being a technical setting: platforms actively optimize ad delivery toward whatever specific objective you select. If you choose "engagement" as your objective, the platform will actively find people statistically likely to like or comment — but that doesn't necessarily mean those same people are likely to actually purchase anything, which is a common, costly beginner mistake worth avoiding entirely.

## Audience Targeting and Segmentation

This is where paid advertising becomes genuinely, dramatically more powerful than organic posting alone. Ad platforms allow precise targeting based on demographics — age, gender, location, right down to a specific city or even neighborhood; interests and behaviors, based on what people already engage with and follow; and, particularly powerful, **custom audiences** — targeting people who have already directly interacted with your business, such as recent website visitors or your existing customer email list, and **lookalike audiences** — new people the platform identifies as closely resembling your existing best customers.

This directly connects back to the audience personas we built in week two: strong, precisely defined personas translate directly into precise, effective ad targeting settings.

## Creative Design for Ads

Ad creative follows many of the same core visual and video principles we covered in week four, with one important addition: a genuinely clear, unmistakable call to action. Every effective ad needs to communicate, quickly and unambiguously, exactly what you want the viewer to do next — visit a website, send a message, make a purchase — and that call to action should be visually and textually obvious, not left for the viewer to guess or infer.

## Budgeting and Bid Strategies

Ad platforms typically offer either **daily budgets**, a set spending amount per day, or **lifetime budgets**, a fixed total amount spread intelligently across a campaign's entire specified duration.

**Bid strategies** determine specifically how a platform spends your allocated budget to reach its stated objective. Automatic bidding lets the platform's own algorithm optimize spending on your behalf, which is generally the sensible, recommended choice for beginners just starting out. Manual bidding gives you direct, granular control, but requires considerably more experience to use effectively without wasting money.

A genuinely important practical principle for beginners: start with a small test budget before committing significant money to any single campaign. This directly connects to the next topic.

## A/B Testing for Ads

**A/B testing**, sometimes called split testing, means running two or more variations of an ad simultaneously — different images, different headlines, different targeting — to determine, with real data, which version genuinely performs better, rather than simply guessing based on personal preference.

Effective A/B testing changes only one variable at a time. If you simultaneously change both the image and the headline text, and performance shifts, you genuinely won't know which specific change actually caused that shift. This is exactly the kind of disciplined, methodical testing you'll practice hands-on in this week's practical exercise.

## Performance Tracking and Optimization

Once ads are live, ongoing monitoring is essential, not optional. Key metrics include **click-through rate**, the percentage of people who saw an ad and actually clicked it; **cost per click** and **cost per conversion**, telling you plainly how efficiently your budget is actually being spent; and **return on ad spend**, comparing the actual revenue generated against total ad spend.

**Optimization** means actively using this ongoing performance data to make continuous, deliberate improvements — pausing consistently underperforming ads, reallocating budget toward whatever is clearly working best, and refining targeting based on real, observed results rather than initial assumptions.
