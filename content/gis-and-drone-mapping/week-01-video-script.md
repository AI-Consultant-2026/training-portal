## What Is GIS?

A **Geographic Information System (GIS)** is **a system designed to capture, store, analyze, and display data that has a location component** — anything you can point to on a map. A GIS can show which farms flood most frequently, or the fastest emergency route between a hospital and any address.

The power of GIS comes from combining location with **attribute data** — additional information like soil quality, water access, and crop yield history layered onto farm boundaries.

A GIS has four core components: **spatial data** itself; **software**, like QGIS, which we use hands-on today; **hardware** — computers, GPS devices, and later, drones; and, just as important as any technical piece, **people** — analysts who know how to ask the right questions and interpret what the data shows.

## Vector vs. Raster

Every piece of spatial data uses one of two models — understanding the difference is essential.

**Vector data** represents features using **precise points, lines, and polygons, defined by exact geographic coordinates.** A well location is a **point**. A road is a **line**. A farm boundary is a **polygon**. Vector stays crisp no matter how far you zoom in.

**Raster data** represents information **as a grid of cells, or pixels, each holding a value** — similar to a digital photograph. **Satellite imagery, elevation data, and rainfall measurements** are typically raster.

The memorable test: if you can clearly answer "where exactly does this feature start and end," it's **vector**. If the answer is "it varies gradually and continuously across the area," it's **raster**.

## Real-World Use Cases

In **agriculture**, GIS combines **soil quality, historical rainfall, and crop yield records** to identify which areas suit particular crops, or where irrigation investment delivers the greatest benefit.

In **water resources**, GIS maps water access points and models **how flooding is likely to spread during heavy rainy seasons** — directly informing where flood defenses are most needed.

In **urban planning**, GIS supports decisions on road networks and where new schools or clinics would serve the most underserved residents, based on real population distribution.

None of these are academic exercises — they're the exact kind of work that supports better decisions by governments, NGOs, and businesses.
